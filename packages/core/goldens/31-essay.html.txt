<h1 id="the-arithmetic-of-the-shore" data-marxy-s="0" data-marxy-e="29">The Arithmetic of the Shore</h1>
<blockquote data-marxy-s="31" data-marxy-e="215">
<p data-marxy-s="33" data-marxy-e="155">The sea keeps a better calendar than we do. It has never once been late, and it has never once been on time by our clocks.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="160" data-marxy-e="215">— from a harbourmaster’s notebook, Saltreach, undated</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="i" data-marxy-s="217" data-marxy-e="221">I</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="223" data-marxy-e="619">The tide gauge at Saltreach stands at the end of the old coal quay, in a brick hut no bigger than a confessional, and for a hundred and forty years someone has gone out to it twice a day with a pencil. I went out to it on a morning in March, at a quarter past six, because I had been told that if I wanted to understand what a tide table was I should first watch a person make one of its numbers.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="621" data-marxy-e="1011">The keeper that year was a woman in her sixties with a flask of tea and a waxed jacket the colour of wet slate. She did not look up when I came in. She was reading a float, which is to say she was watching a brass pointer inch along a scale painted on the inside of a pipe, and there is nothing in the world more like watching a kettle. I stood by the door and tried to look like furniture.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1013" data-marxy-e="1103">“You can sit,” she said, after some minutes. “It does not go faster if you stand.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1105" data-marxy-e="1133">“How long does it take?”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1135" data-marxy-e="1632">“Well, that depends what you mean by <em data-marxy-s="1174" data-marxy-e="1178">it</em>.” She wrote a figure in a ledger that was ruled in columns older than either of us. “If you mean the reading, a few seconds. If you mean the water, six hours and a bit. If you mean the work—” and here she stopped, and drank some tea, and looked at me for the first time. “My predecessor used to say, ‘The work is knowing which of the three you were asked about.’ I have never decided whether that was wisdom or an excuse for being late.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1634" data-marxy-e="2184">I wrote that down. I have it still, in a notebook whose pages are slightly swollen from the damp, and I have come to think that her predecessor had it right, and that the confusion he described is the whole subject of this essay. A tide is an event, a measurement and a prediction at once, and the three have different owners, different tempers and different ways of being wrong. The sea owns the event. The keeper owns the measurement. The prediction belongs to nobody in particular, and so it belongs to everybody who has ever had to catch a ferry.</p>
<h2 id="ii" data-marxy-s="2186" data-marxy-e="2191">II</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="2193" data-marxy-e="2921">Begin with what is not in dispute. Twice in a little over a day, the surface of the sea at any given shore rises and falls, and the interval between one high water and the next is, on average, twelve hours and twenty-five minutes. The odd twenty-five minutes is the first thing a child notices and the last thing a printed table can afford to forget. It exists because the Moon, which does most of the pulling, circles the Earth in the same direction as the Earth turns, so that a point on the coast has to turn a little farther each day to catch up with it. Add the minutes up and the tide arrives some fifty minutes later on each successive day,<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-5" data-marxy-s="2840" data-marxy-e="2844"><a href="#marxy-fn-5">5</a></sup> which is why no one who has lived by the water believes in “the usual time.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="2923" data-marxy-e="3514">The second thing not in dispute is the fortnight. Twice a month, at new Moon and at full Moon, the Sun and the Moon pull along the same line and the tide runs higher and lower than at any other time: these are the spring tides, which have nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the verb, to spring, to leap. A week later, when the two bodies pull at right angles and each partly cancels the other, the range shrinks to its smallest and the water seems almost reluctant. These are the neaps. The word is older than the explanation and nobody now knows where it came from.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-1" data-marxy-s="3510" data-marxy-e="3514"><a href="#marxy-fn-1">1</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="3516" data-marxy-e="4287">What follows from those two facts is a sort of rhythm that people on the shore absorb the way sailors absorb the roll of a deck. Low water in the early morning and high water at supper, and then, a week later, the reverse. A harbour pilot of my acquaintance, retired now, told me that he never once consulted the table for the simple question of whether the water would be high or low at a given hour; he knew, in his body, within a quarter of an hour. What he consulted the table for was the <em data-marxy-s="4009" data-marxy-e="4017">height</em>, because height is where the arithmetic starts to matter: a ship that draws nine metres cannot cross a bar that holds eight and a half, and one hour’s error in a prediction is, near the turn of the tide, almost nothing, while ten centimetres of error can be everything.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="4289" data-marxy-e="5025">Not every shore has this rhythm. In the Mediterranean the tide is so small that for most purposes it is absent, and the people who live there are entitled to think the rest of us are exaggerating. In the Bay of Fundy, at the other extreme, the range between low water and high water can approach sixteen metres,<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-6" data-marxy-s="4600" data-marxy-e="4604"><a href="#marxy-fn-6">6</a></sup> a five-storey building’s worth of sea, and the water comes in over the flats faster than a person can comfortably walk. Between those two extremes lie all the harbours of the world, each with its own constants, its own local grudge. Saltreach, which is where I began, has a mean range of a little over four metres at springs and a little under two at neaps, and it has a habit that I will come to, of holding its breath.</p>
<h2 id="iii" data-marxy-s="5027" data-marxy-e="5033">III</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="5035" data-marxy-e="5742">The gauge itself is not complicated, and its plainness is part of its dignity. A pipe, open at the bottom, is sunk into the sea; a small hole near its base lets water in slowly, so that the waves which slap and surge outside are damped to nothing and only the slow movement of the whole sea is allowed through. This is called a stilling well.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-3" data-marxy-s="5377" data-marxy-e="5381"><a href="#marxy-fn-3">3</a></sup> Inside it, a float rises and falls on a wire, and the wire turns a drum, and the drum, in the old days, wrote a continuous line on a sheet of paper with an ink pen. Today the drum has been replaced by a sensor and the sensor reports to a computer in a cabinet two hundred metres away. But the hut is still there. The keeper still comes. The pencil still moves.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="5744" data-marxy-e="5806">I asked her why, and she gave me an answer I had not expected.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="5808" data-marxy-e="6127">“Because the sensor can be wrong without telling anyone,” she said. “It drifts. It says four point two three and it is four point two seven, and it says it with exactly the same confidence. A person with a pencil and a staff and a good pair of eyes can be wrong too, God knows, but she can be <em data-marxy-s="6107" data-marxy-e="6114">asked</em> about it.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="6129" data-marxy-e="6146">“Asked what?”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="6148" data-marxy-e="6417">“‘Why did you write that?’ And she can say, ‘Because there was a swell and I took the middle of it.’ Or, ‘Because I was cold and I rounded.’ Try asking the sensor.” She closed the ledger. “It is not that machines lie. It is that they never explain.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="6419" data-marxy-e="7014">I have thought about that remark more than I would like. Nothing in it is an argument against instruments; the keeper’s own gauge, she told me, had been calibrated against a levelling benchmark cut into the quay wall in the year 1893, a small brass disc that the surveyors still visit. It is an argument about where the account of a number lives. A reading made by a person has a story attached, even when the story is only “cold, rounded.” A reading made by a sensor has a story too, but it is in a manual, and the manual is in a drawer, and the drawer is in an office that is open on weekdays.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="7016" data-marxy-e="7575">The ledger at Saltreach goes back, with two gaps, to the year 1884. One gap is the winter of 1940, when the hut was struck by something the entry for that week describes only as “damage, see separate report,” and the separate report has not survived. The other is a fortnight in 1962 that no one has been able to explain, though a retired surveyor has a theory involving a flu epidemic and a keeper with no deputy. These gaps are the most-read pages in the book. People come and stand in front of them as if the missing numbers might still be somehow legible.</p>
<h2 id="iv" data-marxy-s="7577" data-marxy-e="7582">IV</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="7584" data-marxy-e="8239">Here is the difficulty the keeper’s ledger exists to solve. The tide is not one wave but a crowd of them. The Moon’s pull has a period of about twelve hours and twenty-five minutes; the Sun’s has a period of exactly twelve hours; the Moon’s orbit is slightly elliptical, so its pull waxes and wanes over a month; it is also tilted, so the pull has a daily component that varies with the season; and the sea itself, sloshing in a basin with a shape of its own, resonates with some of these periods and damps others. A harbour is, in effect, a musical instrument that has been tuned by geography, and the same moonlight plucks it differently on every coast.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="8241" data-marxy-e="9113">The way out, which took the better part of a century to make rigorous, is to refuse to predict the tide directly and to predict its <em data-marxy-s="8373" data-marxy-e="8385">components</em> instead. Each of the principal pulls is assigned a name and a speed. The main lunar tide that recurs about twice a day is called M2, its solar companion S2, the elliptical correction N2,<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-7" data-marxy-s="8572" data-marxy-e="8576"><a href="#marxy-fn-7">7</a></sup> and so on down a list that, for a serious harbour, runs to thirty or forty entries and, for a research station, to well over a hundred. For each component, the keeper’s ledger supplies two numbers: an amplitude, which says how large it is at this harbour, and a phase, which says how far behind the Moon’s own schedule it arrives. Those constants, once found, are the fingerprints of the place. The prediction for any hour of any year is a sum — the heights of all the components at that moment, added to the average level of the sea.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="9115" data-marxy-e="9753">The elegance of this is hard to overstate and easy to forget. Fifty years of readings, laboriously taken twice a day by a succession of people with pencils, are compressed into a table of a few dozen pairs of numbers. And that table can then be used to say what the water will be doing at Saltreach on any afternoon in the next century. The office that issues such tables is, on one stretch of the German coast, called a Wasserstandsvorhersagezentrale,<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-4" data-marxy-s="9567" data-marxy-e="9571"><a href="#marxy-fn-4">4</a></sup> which is longer than most English sentences deserve to be. It is not magic. It is just that the sea, like most large and sullen things, is regular if you ask it the right questions.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="9755" data-marxy-e="10728">The datum is the second half of the discipline. A height is nothing without a zero, and the zero a harbour chooses is a matter of conscience as well as convenience. Charts are drawn to a datum low enough that the sea almost never falls beneath it, so a sailor reading a depth can trust that the real water will be at least as deep as the printed one.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-8" data-marxy-s="10105" data-marxy-e="10109"><a href="#marxy-fn-8">8</a></sup> That is called lowest astronomical tide, LAT. Land surveyors use another zero, a mean level of the sea over many years, MSL, for reasons that have nothing to do with ships. Harbour works are designed against highest astronomical tide, HAT, and against the mean levels of the springs, MHWS and MLWS, which have the sound of a motoring club. And everything is timed in UTC, because a table that said “high water at 14:10, local, except when the clocks go forward” would not survive its first January. These acronyms are ugly and they are, I have come to believe, among the most trustworthy words in the English language.</p>
<h2 id="v" data-marxy-s="10730" data-marxy-e="10734">V</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="10736" data-marxy-e="11484">For a long time the sum was done by hand, and it is worth saying how. To predict the water at one harbour for one hour, a computer (the word meant a person, and meant it for most of its history) took the thirty or forty constants from the ledger, looked up the angle that each component had reached at that hour, found its cosine in a book of tables, multiplied by the amplitude, added all of the results, and added the mean level of the sea. Then she did it again for the next hour. A single year at a single harbour needs about nine thousand such hours, and a single hour needs thirty or forty multiplications, so that a year of printed tide is a few hundred thousand small arithmetic acts, any one of which can quietly spoil everything after it.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="11486" data-marxy-e="12384">The first answer to this was patience, and the second answer was brass. In the later nineteenth century, in several countries at once, people who were tired of long division built machines for the purpose. The principle is easy to say and surprisingly hard to build well. A long wire is fixed at one end and passes, in turn, over a row of pulleys, one pulley for each component of the tide. Each pulley hangs from a small crank, and each crank turns at the speed of its own component, so that the pulley rises and falls with exactly the rhythm of the pull it stands for. The throw of the crank is set to the component’s amplitude, and the crank’s starting angle is set to its phase. At the free end of the wire, a pen touches a roll of paper that moves steadily past it. When the operator turns the handle, the wire adds up the movement of every pulley at once, and the pen draws, in ink, the tide.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="12386" data-marxy-e="12895">I have seen one of these machines. It lives in a glass case in a maritime museum, and, on certain afternoons, a volunteer who is old enough to remember a working one winds it up for visitors. It is a tall, ticking, beautifully pointless-looking object, with the air of a clock that has taken up philosophy. The volunteer told me that a year of tide for one port could be drawn in a day and a half; and that in the room where it was made, the noise was “like a hundred cutlery drawers being opened slowly.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="12897" data-marxy-e="13021">“What did he say about it, the man who ran it?” I asked her. She had inherited the machine’s stories along with its key.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="13023" data-marxy-e="13379">“He said—and I’m quoting my grandfather here, who worked next to him—‘The machine does not think; it adds.’ And then he said, ‘That is the nicest thing you can say about anything.’” She smiled, wiping a brass plate with her sleeve. “My grandfather thought it was the wisest thing he’d ever heard. I thought he was just fond of his job.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="13381" data-marxy-e="13954">I put the question of wisdom aside, because what interested me was the claim itself. A machine that merely adds is a machine that can be audited, and the thing a harbour wants from its table above all is not brilliance but auditability. If the wire says 4.7 metres at nine o’clock, anyone can walk round the case, count the pulleys, check the throw of each crank against the constants in the book and see for themselves. The sum is visible. Every term is in plain view, in brass, with its name stamped on it, and there is nowhere in the mechanism for an assumption to hide.</p>
<h2 id="vi" data-marxy-s="13956" data-marxy-e="13961">VI</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="13963" data-marxy-e="14587">Even a machine that can be audited can be set wrongly, and so the table at Saltreach has never been printed from a single computation. The harbour board’s rule, which was written down in 1911 and has not been amended, is that every printed table must be calculated twice, by two people, in two rooms, who are forbidden to compare notes until both have finished. Only the hours on which they agree go to the printer. The hours on which they disagree are done a third time, by a third person, and the disagreement itself is recorded in a book that is kept for no stated purpose and consulted, as far as I can learn, by nobody.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="14589" data-marxy-e="15202">I asked to see that book, and the clerk who keeps it brought it out with a faint air of apology. It is a plain thing, ruled in pencil, with three columns headed <em data-marxy-s="14750" data-marxy-e="14757">First</em>, <em data-marxy-s="14759" data-marxy-e="14767">Second</em> and <em data-marxy-s="14772" data-marxy-e="14779">Third</em>, and a fourth, headed simply <em data-marxy-s="14809" data-marxy-e="14816">Cause</em>. The <em data-marxy-s="14822" data-marxy-e="14829">Cause</em> column is almost entirely empty. When it is not, the entries are short and human: “carried a one,” “mis-set cylinder 6,” “slept,” “wrong year,” and, in a hand that gets steadily smaller down the page, “see the foot of 1927 … see the foot of 1927 … see the foot of 1927.” There is no foot of 1927 in the volume. There is only the nervous repetition.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="15204" data-marxy-e="15892">It is tempting to dismiss this as the quaintness of a time before spreadsheets. I think the opposite is true. The two-room rule is a stronger guarantee than most of the checks that replaced it, because the check and the thing checked are independent: they do not share an assumption, a library or a bad night’s sleep. When the two computers agree, they agree for reasons that are not the same reason, and that is as close to a proof as a table of tides is ever likely to get. The cost was a full week of two people’s labour for every year printed. The harbour paid it for more than eighty years, and when it finally stopped, it did so with the air of a household giving up the second key.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="15894" data-marxy-e="16492">The pages that most interested me were not the ones with errors but the ones without them: forty or fifty consecutive pages, years on end, in which the <em data-marxy-s="16046" data-marxy-e="16053">Cause</em> column is blank and all three entries are the same figure, to the centimetre, in three different hands. A great deal of human effort went into producing nothing at all, if by nothing one means no disagreement, no news and no event for the clerk to report. It struck me that this is exactly what a good table looks like from the inside. It looks like pages 12–18 of a book, entirely uneventful, and the uneventfulness is the achievement.</p>
<h2 id="vii" data-marxy-s="16494" data-marxy-e="16500">VII</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="16502" data-marxy-e="16725">There is one coast where the arithmetic seems to turn into a story, and since I have not the right to tell it in English, I will let a man in a café in Normandy tell it as he told me, in French, with his coffee going cold.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="16727" data-marxy-e="17480">« La baie du Mont n’est pas une baie comme les autres. À marée basse, on croit que la mer est partie pour toujours — on voit le sable à perte de vue, les pêcheurs à pied, les chiens, les enfants qui courent. Puis, un jour de grande marée, elle revient, et elle ne revient pas doucement. Elle monte à la vitesse, dit-on, d’un cheval au galop ; je ne l’ai jamais vérifié, et je ne connais personne qui l’ait fait. Mais je sais qu’elle arrive en silence, par les chenaux, d’abord, et qu’ensuite le sable entier se met à luire. Ça, c’est vrai. Les touristes sont toujours surpris. Nous, non. Nous consultons le tableau, l’horaire des marées, comme d’autres consultent le journal : par habitude, par précaution, et un peu par superstition. »</p>
<p data-marxy-s="17482" data-marxy-e="17815">He drank, grimaced at the temperature of the coffee, and added in English, for my benefit, “The table is not for the <em data-marxy-s="17601" data-marxy-e="17610">knowing</em>. The table is so that you do not have to be brave.” I was never able to determine whether this was a proverb or something he had just invented, and I decided, on reflection, that it did not matter.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-10" data-marxy-s="17810" data-marxy-e="17815"><a href="#marxy-fn-10">10</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="17817" data-marxy-e="18305">The man had said something exact without intending to. A tide table is not primarily a device for knowing. It is a device for <em data-marxy-s="17943" data-marxy-e="17960">sparing bravery</em>. Everyone who has crossed a tidal flat with the wrong confidence has later understood that there is a middle region between ignorance and certainty, populated by people who did the sum ahead of time so that they would not have to do it in the dark. The table is the sum, done by someone who was not there, handed forward to someone who will be.</p>
<h2 id="viii" data-marxy-s="18307" data-marxy-e="18314">VIII</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="18316" data-marxy-e="18550">At Saltreach, the keepers have a rhyme that is taught to every new deputy, and I give it as it was given to me, with its line breaks,<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-11" data-marxy-s="18449" data-marxy-e="18454"><a href="#marxy-fn-11">11</a></sup> because it loses what it is for when it is run together. The first is the rule for the springs:</p>
<p data-marxy-s="18552" data-marxy-e="18772">New moon, full moon, spring is near,<br data-marxy-s="18588" data-marxy-e="18590" />
water runs both far and clear,<br data-marxy-s="18620" data-marxy-e="18622" />
leaves the mud and leaves the weed,<br data-marxy-s="18657" data-marxy-e="18659" />
comes back running, comes back with speed.<br data-marxy-s="18701" data-marxy-e="18703" />
Check the quay, and check the chain,<br data-marxy-s="18739" data-marxy-e="18741" />
check the mooring, check again.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="18774" data-marxy-e="18926">The second, which I like better, is the rule for the neaps, and it is sung rather than said, slowly, in a minor key that does not exist on any keyboard:</p>
<p data-marxy-s="18928" data-marxy-e="19125">Quarter moon and quiet sea,<br data-marxy-s="18955" data-marxy-e="18958" />
nothing much to say to me,<br data-marxy-s="18984" data-marxy-e="18987" />
shallow, shy and slow to turn,<br data-marxy-s="19017" data-marxy-e="19020" />
neither one thing nor the other;<br data-marxy-s="19052" data-marxy-e="19055" />
sit and drink your tea and learn<br data-marxy-s="19087" data-marxy-e="19090" />
how a small tide holds its brother.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="19127" data-marxy-e="19504">Both stanzas are, as far as I know, anonymous. The keeper told me they were older than the ledger. I asked her what the rhyme was for, since every deputy has the table, and she said: “It is for when you have lost the table. Or when the table has lost you.” I do not know that anything better has ever been said about the relation between a written rule and a memorised one.</p>
<h2 id="ix" data-marxy-s="19506" data-marxy-e="19511">IX</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="19513" data-marxy-e="20326">It is tempting to think of the history of tide prediction as a ladder of improvements, each rung rising neatly on the last. It was not. It was a series of arguments, in which people who disagreed about causes made tables that agreed about heights, and people who agreed about causes made tables that disagreed about heights, and in each case the harbour got on with business regardless. The practical record is more like a map of consolations than a map of discoveries. <p data-marxy-s="21351" data-marxy-e="22175">Two of those rows deserve a pause. The first is 1953, which is the reason the harbour board commissioned its first serious study of the difference between <em data-marxy-s="21506" data-marxy-e="21517">predicted</em> and <em data-marxy-s="21522" data-marxy-e="21532">observed</em> water. A tide table gives the astronomical tide: the part of the sea’s height that follows from the heavens. It does not give the weather. A deep low-pressure system lifts the sea by about a centimetre for each millibar of missing pressure, and a long onshore wind banks the water against the coast like snow against a wall. When the two arrive together, at a spring high water, the sea can exceed its table by a margin the table never promised. The lesson the harbour took from 1953 was not that the table had failed. The lesson was that the table had never claimed to be the whole of the truth, and that no one had read the small print.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-2" data-marxy-s="22171" data-marxy-e="22175"><a href="#marxy-fn-2">2</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="22177" data-marxy-e="22540">The second is 1998, and I put it in because the keeper insisted on it. A younger engineer, she told me, had suggested that the hut might now be closed, and that she might be released to a better life. “I told him I’d consider it the day the sensor could tell me what it had for breakfast,” she said. “He laughed. I don’t believe he thought it was a joke.”</p>
<h2 id="x" data-marxy-s="22542" data-marxy-e="22546">X</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="22548" data-marxy-e="22659">I want to end by describing the one habit of Saltreach that I promised earlier. I called it holding its breath.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="22661" data-marxy-e="23308">Twice a day, at the very top of the tide, the water at the end of the old quay does something that is neither rising nor falling. This is called the stand, and at most harbours it lasts only a few minutes and is hard to detect. At Saltreach, because of the shape of the estuary behind it, the stand runs on for most of an hour. The float sits at the same mark on the scale; the line on the drum goes flat as a table; the keeper, who has been busy for six hours, sets down her pencil. Nothing happens. The longest stand in the book lasted sixty-eight minutes.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-12" data-marxy-s="23219" data-marxy-e="23224"><a href="#marxy-fn-12">12</a></sup> The gulls, who have no tables, appear to know it anyway, and settle along the rail.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="23310" data-marxy-e="23794">She calls it “the interval.” The younger staff call it “the stall,” and the harbour pilots, who are less sentimental, call it a nuisance, because for forty minutes the depth over the bar is as good as it ever gets and the whole fleet wants to leave at once. But I have come to think that the stand is the real subject of the table, in the way that a rest is the real subject of a bar of music. The numbers describe motion. They exist so that a person can know when there will be none.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="23796" data-marxy-e="24270">On the morning I visited, I stayed for the stand. I do not remember what I expected. The keeper stood at the door, her flask at her feet, and said nothing. The float did not move. Somewhere behind us a diesel engine coughed twice and caught. Out on the flats, a man and a dog were making their slow way toward a line of stakes that the sea was about to take away, and they did not hurry, because the table had told them the hour and the table, so far, has never been late…</p>
<p data-marxy-s="24272" data-marxy-e="24297">She picked up the pencil.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="24299" data-marxy-e="24341">“There,” she said. “Now it turns.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="24343" data-marxy-e="24621">I looked at the scale. I could see nothing. I looked again, and then, so slowly that I could not have said which instant it began, the brass pointer had moved down by the width of a fingernail. It was, I realised, the first time in my life I had watched the sea change its mind.</p>
<h2 id="notes" data-marxy-s="24623" data-marxy-e="24631">Notes</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="24633" data-marxy-e="25082">The harbour, the keeper and the verses are invented; the facts about the tide are not. The rhymes are the author’s own. A harbour called Saltreach does not exist, and neither does its ledger, though the habit of holding the pencil still while the sea stands is real at more than one of the places that suggested it. The long compound and the long address that appear in the notes below are there on purpose, since a typeset page has to survive them.</p>
