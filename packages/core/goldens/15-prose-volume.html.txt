<h1 data-marxy-s="74" data-marxy-e="102">A winter of borrowed rooms</h1>
<p data-marxy-s="104" data-marxy-e="675">I have spent most of my reading life in rooms that were not built for
it. Kitchens with a radio left low. Clinic chairs that face a television
no one watches. The spare room of a house whose owners have gone to the
coast and left the heating on a timer. A train that should have arrived
before midnight and did not. The page has to live in all of those
places, and I have come to think that this is not a defect in the life
around the book — it is the ordinary condition of reading, and any
account of a page that pretends otherwise is describing a furniture
catalogue.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="677" data-marxy-e="1096">That is a stronger claim than it sounds. It means the sentence has to
survive interruption. It means a paragraph is not a chapel. It means the
white at the right-hand edge of a column of type is the least of what a
line has to put up with. A child asks a question. A conductor wants a
ticket. The kettle clicks off and you get up, and when you come back the
sentence has not moved, and that is its only reliable virtue.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1098" data-marxy-e="1152">I am not writing a theory. I am writing down a winter.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1154" data-marxy-e="1831">The winter I mean ran from the first hard frost in November to a thaw
that arrived without announcing itself, sometime in the last week of
February, when the estuary stopped looking like metal and started
looking like water again. I was staying, on and off, in other people’s
houses, because my own lease had ended and the next one had not begun,
and because two friends had said, independently, that the room at the
back was empty and I should use it. I took both offers. I packed one bag
that was mostly books and one bag that was mostly shirts, and I learned,
again, that a life can be made to fit into less than you think if you
are willing to reread what you already have.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="1833" data-marxy-e="2091">What follows is an account of the rooms, and of the pages that had to
hold their shape inside them. I have changed a few names and left the
weather alone. The quotations are as I remember them, which is to say
they are as true as memory ever is, and no more.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="2093" data-marxy-e="2106">The clinic</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="2108" data-marxy-e="2614">The clinic on Harbour Street keeps its magazines in a rack that has been
repaired with parcel tape. Nobody reads them. People pick them up, open
them at a random page, and hold them the way you hold a ticket you do
not need yet. I had a paperback in my coat — a short novel I had already
finished once and was walking through again, the way you walk a path in
winter to see which branches came down. I was there for a blood test
that would, the letter had promised, take ten minutes and take all
morning.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="2616" data-marxy-e="3037">A woman sat across from me with an envelope already open in her lap. She
was not hiding that she was rereading it. Every so often she would fold
it along a crease that already existed, as if the paper had learned her
hands, and then unfold it again. When she caught me looking she did not
look away. She said, as if we had already been talking, “They write as
if the news were a kindness if they space the sentences out.”</p>
<p data-marxy-s="3039" data-marxy-e="3443">I did not know what to say to that, so I said nothing, which she seemed
to accept as an answer. The television on the wall was showing a cooking
programme with the sound off. A man in a white jacket sprinkled salt
from a height, and the salt fell through a silence that the waiting room
supplied for free. Somewhere behind a door a printer started and
stopped. My name was not called. I opened the novel.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="3445" data-marxy-e="4013">The second time through a book is when you notice the furniture. The
first time you are following someone through a house at night; the
second time you see which chairs they always pass and which doors they
never open. I was in a chapter that takes place almost entirely in a
hallway — a conversation that keeps being interrupted by people who need
to get past — and I thought, sitting under the cooking programme, that
the novelist had understood waiting rooms without ever setting a scene
in one. The hallway was a waiting room. The interruptions were the
point.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="4015" data-marxy-e="4324">A nurse opened the inner door and read a name that was not mine. The
woman with the letter did not move. I turned a page and the cheap
binding crackled, a sound out of proportion to the action, and two
people glanced over as if I had spoken. I wanted to tell them it was
only glue, aging in public. I did not.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="4326" data-marxy-e="4891">There is a particular shame in reading a book you have already finished
while other people are receiving news. It feels like bringing a picnic
to a census. And yet the alternative — to sit with empty hands and
perform patience — has never seemed more honest to me, only more
visible. I have watched people in this same row of chairs scroll until
their faces go slack, and I have watched people stare at the salt
falling on the muted television, and I have decided that a reread is at
least a form of attention that knows what it is attending
to.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-1" data-marxy-s="4875" data-marxy-e="4891"><a href="#marxy-fn-1">1</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="4893" data-marxy-e="5499">When my name was finally called I put the novel face down on the chair,
which is a superstition I cannot defend: as if the opened pages might
hold my place in the room as well as in the story. The phlebotomist had
cold hands and a voice trained to be unmemorable. “Make a fist. Look at
the far wall if you like.” I looked at the far wall. There was a poster
about washing your hands, and someone had drawn, very lightly, a small
boat in the corner of the sink. I thought about the woman and her letter
and about the way a sentence can be spaced out until it stops being a
blow and becomes a weather report.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="5501" data-marxy-e="6059">Afterwards I walked down to the estuary because the cotton wool in the
elbow needed a destination. The water was the colour of an unpolished
spoon. A man was throwing bread that the gulls could not be bothered to
fight over. I stood there longer than the cotton wool required, and I
understood, not for the first time, that I had come to the clinic with a
book so that I would have something to return to that was not my own
blood in a vial with a printed label. That is a small and ordinary
cowardice. I record it because the winter was made of such things.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="6061" data-marxy-e="6090">The kitchen after midnight</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="6092" data-marxy-e="6568">Mara’s kitchen faced the alley, not the water, which she said was the
only reason the rent was survivable. The window looked onto a brick wall
and a drainpipe that rang when it rained. She had gone north for three
weeks to sit with her father, and she had left me a note on the table
under a jar of coffee: “Eat whatever will go off. The good plates are
the chipped ones. Do not try to be a guest.” I tried to be a guest for
two days and then, as she had predicted, I stopped.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="6570" data-marxy-e="7073">At night the house made a different set of noises than it made in the
day. The refrigerator had a conversation with itself. A pipe ticked as
it cooled. Once, around one in the morning, a fox screamed in the alley
and I stood at the sink with a glass of water and waited to see if it
would do it again. It did. I wrote that down on the back of a receipt,
for no reason except that the sound had a shape and I wanted a record of
the shape. In the morning the receipt looked melodramatic. I kept it
anyway.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="7075" data-marxy-e="7810">I read at the table because the spare room’s lamp had a shade that made
everything sepia, and because the kitchen was the only place in the
house that still felt as if someone lived there. Mara’s handwriting was
on a shopping list stuck to the fridge — oat milk, oranges, the cheap
wine not the other cheap wine — and her coat was on the chair I did not
use. A house from which the owner has only temporarily gone is more
intimate than an empty house. You are surrounded by decisions that are
still in force. The spices are in the order she thinks of them. The
third drawer sticks and she has not fixed it, and so you do not fix it
either, out of a respect that would be hard to explain to anyone who has
not stayed in such a room.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="7812" data-marxy-e="8363">The book I had on those nights was a collection of letters between two
people who almost married and then, with great courtesy, did not. I had
bought it second-hand with someone else’s train ticket still serving as
a bookmark at a page where nothing particular happens. That seemed
right. Most of a correspondence is the weather of two minds, not the
lightning. I would read four or five letters and then get up and wash
the one plate I had used, because Mara’s instruction not to be a guest
had not, in my reading of it, extended to leaving evidence.</p>
<blockquote data-marxy-s="8365" data-marxy-e="8753">
<p data-marxy-s="8367" data-marxy-e="8753">You asked whether I still walk to the river in the mornings. I do,
though the path has been fenced where the bank slipped last spring,
and so I stand at the fence and look at the water as if looking were
the same as being beside it. I think of you when the light is that
thin colour it goes just before it gives up. I do not know if that is
allowed. I have decided not to ask.</p>
</blockquote>
<p data-marxy-s="8755" data-marxy-e="9324">I copied that passage onto the same pad I used for the fox, and then I
felt foolish, because it was not my letter and not my river. What I
wanted, I think, was proof that a sentence could carry a whole withheld
life without raising its voice. The fence, the thin colour, the decision
not to ask — those are the tools. A person who sets type would talk
about the rag and the measure. A person sitting in a borrowed kitchen at
ten past one talks about whether the writer looked at the water or only
needed the water to be there so the feeling would have a place to sit.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="9326" data-marxy-e="9926">Some nights I did not read at all. I sat with the window black and the
drainpipe ticking and I let the house happen around me. This is also a
kind of reading. You learn the grammar of a place that is not yours:
which floorboard announces you, which tap needs a second twist, which
neighbour goes to bed late enough that their light crosses the alley and
lays a pale bar across the table. I came to like that bar. When it was
absent I knew the neighbour had gone away or given up, and the kitchen
felt more completely borrowed, which is to say more completely mine,
which is a thought I did not enjoy.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="9928" data-marxy-e="10623">On the Thursday before Mara came back I cooked a proper meal, as if the
house required a ceremony of return. I used the chipped plates. I opened
the cheap wine, the one she had meant. I set a place for myself and a
place I did not use, which was theatrical and I knew it, and I ate
slowly because the letters in the book were slowing down too — the two
writers had begun to leave longer gaps, and the editor had started
putting dates in brackets, those little asides that mean a season has
passed in silence. I thought about leaving Mara a letter of my own and
then did not, because anything I could have said would have been a
performance of gratitude, and she had asked me not to be a guest.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="10625" data-marxy-e="10690">She came back on the Sunday. The house remembered her in an hour.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="10692" data-marxy-e="10717">The twenty-three forty</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="10719" data-marxy-e="11228">The last train from the city was supposed to be a convenience and was,
most nights, a rehearsal for patience. It left from a platform that
smelled of wet wool and orange peel. The indicator board had a habit of
revising the future in increments of four minutes. I stood under it and
read, or pretended to read, which in a station is almost the same
activity. People glanced at the cover of whatever you were holding the
way they glance at a stranger’s shopping — not to know you, only to
complete a picture.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="11230" data-marxy-e="11766">I had, that month, a fat paperback whose spine had already given up. The
pages fanned when I opened it, and I had to hold it with both thumbs,
like a bird that might decide to leave. It was not a fashionable book.
It was a long account of a walk from one end of an island to the other,
written by someone who had done the walk twice and could not stop
comparing the two crossings. I liked it for its refusal to arrive. Every
chapter ended on a ridge or a ferry slip, and the next chapter began by
admitting that the weather had changed.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="11768" data-marxy-e="12378">When the train finally accepted us it was three carriages instead of
five, and the heat was on in only one of them. I sat opposite a man who
was trying to finish a crossword in a newspaper that kept sliding on the
table. He said “seven down is a kind of boat” to no one, and then, when
I did not help him, to me. I said I did not do crosswords. He nodded as
if I had confessed a larger failing and went back to the sliding paper.
The window beside us was a black mirror. Beyond it, I knew, were the
sheds and the river and the back of a supermarket, but the glass offered
only our own faces, tired and doubled.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="12380" data-marxy-e="12880">There is no good light on a late train. The overheads are the colour of
weak tea. If you sit under them long enough your page begins to look as
if it has been stored in a cupboard. I have tried the little book lights
that clip on, and I have tried the phone torch, and both of them turn
you into a person performing reading for the carriage. Better, I think,
to let the weak tea settle and to accept that some lines will have to be
read twice. A sentence that survives that light has earned its keep.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="12882" data-marxy-e="13459">Halfway home the train stopped for a reason no one announced. The
crossword man looked up, then down, then folded the paper with a
precision that suggested he had folded papers in stalled trains before.
A child in the next bay asked if they were there yet and was told,
kindly, “Not yet, and not for a while.” I have heard worse definitions
of a life. I went back to the island and to the walker’s second
crossing, in which the same headland looked smaller because he knew it,
and I thought about how a reread is just that: the headland smaller, the
weather differently honest.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="13461" data-marxy-e="14077">When we moved again the carriage made the sound a carriage makes when it
has been asked to remember its job — a gathering of small metallic
agreements. Someone’s bottle rolled and was captured. The crossword man
said, without looking at me, “Ketch. Seven down. I knew it was a kind of
boat.” I told him I was glad. He seemed, for the first time, as if he
might smile, and then did not, which I respected. We were not going to
become people who talked. We were going to share a table until the
estuary towns began to appear, one after another, each with its small
bright station and its dark immediately afterwards.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="14079" data-marxy-e="14726">I got off two stops before the end of the line. The platform was wet and
the ticket gates were open, which is the town’s way of admitting that at
that hour it cannot be bothered to enforce itself. I walked the long way
because the short way passed a pub that would still be loud, and I was
carrying a book whose spine could not take more weather. The streets
were empty in the particular way of a place that has already decided
tomorrow will be ordinary. A cat crossed ahead of me with a sense of
appointment. I said good night to it, which is the sort of thing you do
only when you are sure no person is there to hear the tone you use on
animals.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="14728" data-marxy-e="15402">At the door of the house I was then staying in — not Mara’s, the other
one, the one with the timer on the heating — I stood on the step and
finished the paragraph I was in, under the porch light, because I have a
superstition about carrying an unfinished sentence over a threshold at
night. The walker on the island was describing a bothy with no glass in
the windows, and how he had slept there on the first crossing and only
sat, awake, on the second, because he already knew what the dawn would
look like and wanted to watch it arrive rather than be surprised by it.
I closed the book on that, and went in, and the heating clicked as if it
had been waiting for a cue.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="15404" data-marxy-e="15469">The bothy stayed in my head for days. Some rooms you only sit in.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="15471" data-marxy-e="15488">The spare room</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="15490" data-marxy-e="16072">The second house belonged to people I will call the Ellisons, though
that is not their name and they will never read this. They had gone to
the coast — a different coast from ours, a coast with a brochure — and
they had said I should use the back room and feed the fish, which were
the sort of bright, improbable fish that look designed rather than born.
The tank hummed. At night it threw a blue rectangle on the ceiling, and
I slept under that rectangle and woke to it, and for the first week I
thought of it as a kindness and for the second as a surveillance I had
agreed to.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="16074" data-marxy-e="16607">The spare room had a shelf of books chosen, I came to see, to describe
the guest the Ellisons imagined. There were two novels that had been
prizes a decade ago. There was a history of salt. There was a slim
volume of poems with an uncracked spine, which is the saddest state a
book can be in. There was a guide to the local birds in which someone —
not recently — had ticked the robin, the blackbird, the herring gull,
and then lost heart, or lost the winter, or lost the habit of looking
up. I added nothing. It was not my list.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="16609" data-marxy-e="17317">I did take the history of salt to bed, because the prize novels had the
air of having been discussed at dinners I was not invited to, and I
prefer a book that does not expect me to have been in the room. Salt, it
turns out, is a story about roads and preservation and the fear of rot —
which is to say it is a story about time, which every good history is. I
read about caravans and taxes and the way a white crystal can decide
whether a city lives inland or by the water. The fish tank hummed its
agreement. Sometimes a fish would hang in the blue as if it were
thinking, and I would look up from a sentence about a medieval warehouse
and feel briefly, ridiculously, that we were engaged in the same work.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="17319" data-marxy-e="18021">Guests leave traces they do not intend. I found, in the drawer of the
bedside table, a hairpin and a paperclip and a postcard that had never
been sent. The postcard showed a hotel with flags, and on the back
someone had written only “The breakfast is as they said —” and then
stopped, as if the dash were a cliff. I put it back exactly as it had
been, angled against the side of the drawer. I have thought about that
unfinished sentence more than about most finished books. A person had
sat in this room, or in a room like it, and had begun to tell someone
about breakfast, and the day had taken them away from the telling. The
dash is still there, waiting for a clause that will not come.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-2" data-marxy-s="18010" data-marxy-e="18021"><a href="#marxy-fn-2">2</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="18023" data-marxy-e="18638">The Ellisons had left a note about the fish that was longer than a note
about fish needs to be. It had the tone of people who love a small
system and want the system to outlast a fortnight. Water here, flakes
there, the heater must not be adjusted, the light is on a timer and you
must not override the timer even if you think the room is gloomy. I
obeyed as if the note were a lease. In the evenings I sat in the chair
that was not quite comfortable and I read, and the tank did its blue
work, and I understood that I was part of the system now — a temporary
component, rated for three weeks, not to be adjusted.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="18640" data-marxy-e="19176">One afternoon a neighbour knocked because a parcel had been left with
them. I signed for a box that did not belong to me and put it in the
hall, and the neighbour, who had kind eyes and a dog that leaned against
my shin, said, “You’re the one in the back room. They said you’d be
quiet.” I said I was trying. She laughed, not unkindly, and told me the
last guest had played the piano at hours the street did not agree with.
“You’re already an improvement,” she said, which is a sentence that can
be lived with, if you do not examine it.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="19178" data-marxy-e="19991">I did not play the piano. I walked through the rooms I had not been told
I could use, which is a form of trespass so mild it barely has a name,
and I looked at the photographs on the dresser: a mountain, a wedding, a
child holding a fish that was not one of the bright ones. Other people’s
lives arranged to be glanced at. I did not open drawers in those rooms.
There is a line, and the unsent postcard had already taken me up to it.
I went back to the spare room and to the salt and to the poems I finally
opened, on the last Sunday, out of a sense of duty to the uncracked
spine. They were better than I had wanted them to be. I left the spine
with a single white line, a scar of having been read once, which seemed
the least I could do for a book that had waited on a guest shelf to be
someone’s idea of taste.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="19993" data-marxy-e="20529">When the Ellisons returned I had a story ready about the fish, who had
not died, and about the parcel, which was still in the hall, and about
nothing else. They thanked me with the slightly startled warmth of
people who have been on a brochure coast and have forgotten the exact
dimensions of their own house. I walked out with my bag of books and my
bag of shirts and I did not look up at the back window, because I knew
the blue rectangle would be gone in the daytime, and I did not want to
see the room returned to being only a room.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="20531" data-marxy-e="20568">A letter I was not meant to finish</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="20570" data-marxy-e="21099">In the third week of January a letter arrived for me at Mara’s address,
forwarded by a landlord who still had a stamp and a conscience. It was
from my cousin, who lives in a city I left on purpose and who writes on
paper because she says a screen makes her cruel. The envelope had been
rained on. The ink of my name had run into a small weather of its own. I
stood in Mara’s hall — I was back there by then, between houses — and I
opened it with a thumb, which is an inelegant way to open anything that
has survived the post.</p>
<blockquote data-marxy-s="21101" data-marxy-e="22138">
<p data-marxy-s="21103" data-marxy-e="22138">I keep walking past the cafe where you used to sit with that notebook,
and it is still there, which I resent. The new people have put plants
in the window and the plants are thriving, which I resent more. I went
in last Tuesday and ordered what you used to order, and it was only a
drink, and I sat at the wrong table because your table was taken by a
man who was marking essays with a red pen and a kind of fury I almost
admired. I wanted to tell him that the work will not love him back. I
did not, because I am trying, as you asked, not to narrate other
people’s lives for them. I am narrating yours instead, which is worse,
and I know it, and I am doing it anyway because the city is too full
of you now that you are not in it. Come back or do not come back. I am
not in a position to make that a clean sentence. I am only in a
position to say that the river froze at the edges this week, and I
thought of the winter you said you needed, and I wondered whether it
had arrived, and whether it was enough.</p>
</blockquote>
<p data-marxy-s="22140" data-marxy-e="22702">I sat down on the stairs to finish it, which is a bad place to read a
letter that is trying to be several letters at once. Mara was out. The
house had the mid-afternoon quiet of a place that believes itself empty.
I read the page twice, and then I read the last lines a third time,
because “whether it was enough” is a clause that does not let you put
the paper down cleanly. I had asked for a winter. I had not asked her to
keep a seat at a cafe I had already left. The plants in the window
seemed, at that distance, like an accusation I had no right to answer.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="22704" data-marxy-e="23220">There is a cruelty in being remembered accurately. She had the notebook
right, and the order, and the river. She had the thing I had said about
needing a season that would not flatter me. I had said it standing on a
bridge with a wind that made speech into a kind of work, and I had meant
it, and I had also meant, without saying so, that I wanted someone to
notice I was leaving. She had noticed. She had written it down. The
letter was the bill for that noticing, arrived late and rain-stained, as
bills often are.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="23222" data-marxy-e="23961">I did not write back that day. I put the letter in the book I was
reading, which was a habit I had promised myself I would break, because
a letter used as a bookmark is a way of refusing to file a feeling. For
a week it marked a chapter about a frozen harbour in someone else’s
century, and then I took it out and put it in the inside pocket of the
coat I wore to the late train, as if the cousin’s sentences needed to
travel. They did not do any work there. They only waited, the way the
postcard in the Ellisons’ drawer was waiting, except that this dash was
mine to finish, and I knew it, and I still walked around with it
unfinished, which is a kind of answer and a kind of failure and, in a
winter of borrowed rooms, typical.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-3" data-marxy-s="23952" data-marxy-e="23961"><a href="#marxy-fn-3">3</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="23963" data-marxy-e="24556">When I did write, I wrote too little. I said the winter had arrived. I
said it was not a clean sentence either. I said I had stood by an
estuary that was not her river and had thought of the cafe without
resenting the plants. I did not say I had read her letter on the stairs
like a person in a novel, because she would have liked that too much,
and I did not say come and see the estuary, because I was not sure the
rooms I was in could bear another person. The reply went into a red
postbox that had a seagull perched on it, which felt like a detail I
should not invent and therefore report.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="24558" data-marxy-e="24597">The library that closed on Thursdays</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="24599" data-marxy-e="25222">Our town library is a converted chapel with a damp problem and a
librarian who has decided not to be defeated by either. She is called
Helen when she is wearing her own coat and Mrs. Reed when she is behind
the desk, and I have never been able to establish which name she
prefers, so I use neither and she, in return, calls me the one with the
returns. The building smells of paper and of the heater that sits under
the west window and ticks like a patient clock. On Thursdays it is
closed, which I forgot more than once, arriving with a bag of books to
find the door holding a handwritten sign that said, simply, Thursday.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="25224" data-marxy-e="25920">When it is open, the light comes in through glass that was chosen for a
congregation and is too honest for readers. You sit in it and you are
seen. I liked the corner by the local-history shelves, not because I was
working on local history, but because those volumes are tall enough to
make a small room around a chair. I would take down a parish register
facsimile I had no claim on and rest it closed on the table, as a wall,
and behind it I would read whatever I had actually come for.
Helen-or-Mrs. Reed knew. She once said, passing, “The dead don’t mind
being furniture.” I have been turning that sentence over ever since. It
is either a kindness or a theology. In a chapel it might be both.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="25922" data-marxy-e="26509">A library is the only borrowed room that expects you. The chair is not a
favour. The heat, such as it is, is not a timer you must not touch. You
may take a book that someone else has held and will hold again, and the
transaction is designed so that none of you have to meet. I have always
loved that anonymity, and I have also, this winter, been suspicious of
it. To pass a book through a desk is to pretend that reading is a civic
act rather than a private hunger. It is both, of course. The civic act
is the building. The hunger is what you do in the corner behind the
parish register.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="26511" data-marxy-e="27124">I was there on a Tuesday when the school let out and the children’s
section became a weather system. A boy sat on the floor and narrated, at
full volume, a book about a fox to a younger child who may or may not
have been his sister. He did all the voices. The fox was swaggering; the
chickens were unimpressed. Helen-or-Mrs. Reed did not shush him. She
stood with a trolley and listened as if this were the work, which it
was. I thought of the fox in Mara’s alley, and of the receipt I had
written on, and I felt the two foxes try to become one fox and fail,
which is what images do when you ask too much of them.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="27126" data-marxy-e="27884">Later that afternoon an old man at the computer asked for help with a
form, and the whole desk became a quiet theatre of passwords and
forgotten addresses. I could not hear the details and did not want to.
What I heard was the tone — the librarian lowering her voice not to hide
the information but to give the man a room inside the room. I have seen
priests do that, and I have seen nurses do that, and I have rarely seen
it done better than it was done next to a stack of reserved thrillers.
When the man left he said thank you in the old way, the way that means
the thanks has weight, and she said, “That’s what it’s for,” which might
have been about the computer or the building or the whole idea of a desk
that you can approach without an appointment.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="27886" data-marxy-e="28515">I borrowed, that Tuesday, a book of essays by a woman who had lived for
a year in a lighthouse, not as a keeper but as a person who had asked to
be left somewhere with a rotating light. I thought it would be about
solitude and it was, but it was also about maintenance — the oil, the
lens, the walkway in a wind that wanted you off it. She wrote about the
light as a sentence that kept being said to the water, whether or not
anyone was positioned to receive it. I sat behind my parish-register
wall and I underlined nothing, because it was a library book, and I
remembered instead, which is a poorer system and the one I have.</p>
<blockquote data-marxy-s="28517" data-marxy-e="28950">
<p data-marxy-s="28519" data-marxy-e="28950">The light does not care whether a ship is there. That was the first
thing I had to learn, and the last thing I wanted to. I had come to
the rock for a conversation with my own quiet, and I found instead a
machine for being ignored by the weather. I came to love it for that.
A person can live beside a faithfulness that is not personal. Most of
us already do. We simply prefer our lighthouses to look like other
people.</p>
</blockquote>
<p data-marxy-s="28952" data-marxy-e="29680">I returned the essays a day late and paid the fine in coins, which made
the transaction feel like a century I had not lived in. Helen-or-Mrs.
Reed wrote the amount in a little book, though the computer already
knew. “We keep both,” she said, when she saw me looking. “The machine
forgets in its own way. This forgets in ours.” I walked out under the
honest chapel light and down the steps that still have a groove from the
congregation, and I thought that a library is a lighthouse that lets you
come inside, and that this is almost too neat a thought, and that I
would keep it anyway, the way I kept the receipt with the fox on it,
because a winter is a collection of almost-too-neat thoughts that you
were cold enough to need.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="29682" data-marxy-e="29725">What a sentence does when it has to turn</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="29727" data-marxy-e="30343">I said I was not writing a theory, and I am still not, but I cannot talk
about a winter of pages without talking about the moment a line runs out
of room. You reach the right-hand edge — in a book, in a window of a
train, in the column of a cheap paperback whose margins were decided by
someone who would never ride the twenty-three forty — and the sentence
has to decide whether to break, and where, and whether the break will
look like a choice or like an accident. Readers do not, as a rule,
notice a good decision. They notice a hole. They notice a word left
alone on a line like a person left on a platform.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="30345" data-marxy-e="31063">I notice more than I used to, which is a professional deformation I
acquired by caring about rooms. Once you have sat in a kitchen that
belongs to someone else, you start to see how furniture has been asked
to do jobs it was not bought for. A chair piled with post. A table that
is also a desk that is also a place to put a letter you are not ready to
answer. A page is furniture of that kind. It was made for a reader in a
steady chair, and it is asked, daily, to perform in motion, in queues,
in beds that are not your bed. The line-break is the small courtesy the
page can still offer: I will not leave you with a hole if I can help it.
I will not push a word into the margin as if the margin were not a
wall.<sup class="marxy-footnote-ref" id="marxy-fnref-4" data-marxy-s="31057" data-marxy-e="31063"><a href="#marxy-fn-4">4</a></sup></p>
<p data-marxy-s="31065" data-marxy-e="31590">This is why I have always distrusted the idea that a beautiful page is a
page you look at. A beautiful page is a page you forget you are looking
at, because nothing in the rag has called you out of the sentence, and
nothing in the spacing has made you work for a rhythm that should have
been given. The clinic television, the train heat, the fish-tank blue —
those will interrupt you anyway. The page’s job is not to compete with
them. The page’s job is to be the one surface in the room that does not
ask you to manage it.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="31592" data-marxy-e="32378">I tested this, without meaning to, on the late train, with the book
whose spine had failed. Because I had to hold it with both thumbs, I
turned pages less often, and I stayed longer in each opening, and I
began to see the right-hand edge as a coastline. Some pages were a
kindly shore, the lines arriving at almost the same sand. Some were a
cliff and a rubble of short lines, and my eye kept tripping, and I would
look up into the black window and lose the walker’s ridge. The
difference was not the writer’s. The sentences were the same sentences.
The difference was how the lines had been asked to end. I do not know
who decided those endings — some program, some tired compositor, some
default no one had looked at since the file was made — but I felt the
decision in my thumbs.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="32380" data-marxy-e="33066">A person who has never had to care about this will say that I am
describing nothing, or describing a luxury. They will say that the story
is the thing, and the type is a glass you drink it from, and you do not
praise the glass. I have said this myself, in rooms where it was the
polite thing. I no longer believe it. I have drunk from too many chipped
glasses this winter — I mean this literally, in Mara’s kitchen — and I
know the difference between a chip you forget and a chip that catches
your lip every time. A short line in the wrong place is a chip. A column
that pushes past its measure is a lip of glass you did not expect. You
can still drink. You will remember the glass.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="33068" data-marxy-e="33845">The walker on the island, who compared his two crossings, would
understand this without the metaphor. The path was the same path. The
second time, he knew where the bog began, and he stepped differently,
and the island — which had not changed — became a different walk. A
paragraph is an island you cross more than once if the book is any good.
The line-breaks are the places you put your feet. If they are chosen all
at once, with a view of the whole ridge, you arrive less tired than if
each step was only the next dry-looking tuft. I know how this sounds. I
am going to let it sound that way. A winter of other people’s rooms
teaches you that the humble systems — timers, notes about fish, the way
a paragraph gives up its last line — are where the life actually is.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="33847" data-marxy-e="34434">Helen-or-Mrs. Reed, who keeps two records of a late fine, would
understand it too. The machine forgets in its own way. The little book
forgets in ours. A page that has been set with care is the little book.
It is not more true than the machine. It is differently answerable. You
can see the hand, even when the hand was a set of rules someone chose
and then stood by. I want, in the rooms I have not yet borrowed, to read
pages that were stood by. That is the whole of the theory I swore I
would not write. It fits in a sentence. The rest of this is only the
weather the sentence needed.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="34436" data-marxy-e="34475">Weather, and the days I did not read</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="34477" data-marxy-e="35094">Not every day of the winter had a page in it. I want that on record,
because an account of reading can start to sound like a life made of
reading, which mine is not. There were days of paid work at a table that
was sometimes Mara’s and sometimes a corner of a cafe that was not my
cousin’s cafe, and on those days the sentences I produced were the wrong
kind — useful, forgettable, gone by evening. There were days of walking
because the rooms had become too much room, and I went out along the sea
wall with my hands in my pockets and no book, on purpose, and watched
the tide decide what to do with a row of weed.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="35096" data-marxy-e="35766">The weather was not dramatic, which disappointed a part of me that had
wanted the winter to look like a winter in a novel. We had frost that
made the estuary grass stand up like a brushed pelt. We had rain that
arrived sideways and found the gap in every collar. We had a week of
stillness in January when the smoke from the chimneys went straight up
and hung, and the town smelled of wood that had been stored badly. We
did not have snow that stayed. We had snow that visited in the night and
was gone by the time the school opened, leaving only a wetness that
could have been rain, and a few people saying, at the shop, that their
bins had worn white hats for an hour.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="35768" data-marxy-e="36271">On the still week I went to the sea wall every morning because the house
I was in — the Ellisons’ still, then — felt as if it were holding its
breath with the rest of the town. The fish tank hummed. The timer did
its work. I stood at the wall and looked at a horizon that was a single
unbroken stroke, no ships, no weather worth a name, and I understood the
lighthouse essayist’s point without having to be on a rock. A
faithfulness that is not personal. The water kept arriving. I was not
required.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="36273" data-marxy-e="37088">I met, on one of those mornings, a man I vaguely knew from the shop, and
we stood together in the way of people who have not planned to talk. He
said the stillness made his dog anxious. I said it made the chimneys
look like they were thinking. He laughed, and then we both looked at the
water so the laugh would have somewhere to go. “My wife reads in the
mornings,” he said, after a bit. “I can’t do it. I have to be on my feet
or I start making lists.” I told him I read in other people’s kitchens
at night, which was more than he had asked for, and he nodded as if this
were a known sport with rules. “Each to their own borrowed hour,” he
said, and I have given him that line more credit than a remark on a sea
wall maybe deserves, because it named the winter more cheaply and more
accurately than I had managed.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="37090" data-marxy-e="37709">The days I did not read were not empty. They were full of the unbooked
work of being a person in a town: the queue, the bin, the email that had
to be answered in a voice that was not the voice of these pages. I
mention them so that the rooms do not look like a retreat. They were a
way of staying in the world without having a fixed address in it. A
borrowed room is not an escape from ordinary life. It is ordinary life
with someone else’s pictures on the dresser and someone else’s fish to
keep alive. The reading happened in the gaps, which is where reading has
always happened, even for people who own their chairs.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="37711" data-marxy-e="38405">I did, on a Friday I had marked as a day without pages, pick up the
poems in the Ellisons’ spare room and read three of them standing up, as
if standing made it not count. One of them was about a coat left on a
train, and it was short, and it ended on a dash — not a full stop, a
dash — and I closed the book and went out and did the shopping and came
back and read it again, still standing, because a dash at the end of a
poem is a kind of porch light, and I have already admitted what I am
like about thresholds. The poet had trusted the unfinished thing. I put
the book back with its new white line and I went to feed the fish, who
did not care about dashes, and that, too, was a relief.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="38407" data-marxy-e="38433">Two more rooms, briefly</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="38435" data-marxy-e="39253">There was a week in a hostel in the city, between Mara’s and the
Ellisons’, that I have almost left out because it does not flatter the
shape of this account. The room had four beds and I used one of them for
three nights, and the other three were occupied by a rotation of people
who packed with head-torches and whispered in languages I could not
join. I read with a torch of my own, under a blanket, like a child, and
I hated the performance of it, and I did it anyway because the ceiling
light was a punishment and the alternative was the glow of four phones.
A man in the far bed was listening to a lecture with one earphone in,
and phrases escaped: “the surplus,” “as we saw in week four,” “any
questions so far.” I never learned the subject. I learned only that
someone else’s education can become your weather.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="39255" data-marxy-e="40026">The hostel had a kitchen that was a more desperate version of Mara’s:
the same problem — how to be a person among other people’s jars — solved
with less grace and more labelling. I cooked pasta in a pan that had
been left wet, and I ate it standing, and I read two pages of the island
walk taped to the tiled wall with my eyes, which is not a way I
recommend. Then I went up the stairs that smelled of someone else’s
shampoo and I lay in the dark and I thought about all the rooms, and I
had a moment of wanting a room that was mine, with a lamp I had chosen,
and I let the want sit, and then I let it go, because the winter was not
for settling. The winter was for finding out what a page could survive.
A hostel blanket is a severe test. The page survived. I slept.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="40028" data-marxy-e="40835">There was also, for two afternoons only, the back room of the bookshop
on the high street, where I sat because the owner had said I could if I
bought something, and I bought something, and then I felt I had paid for
a chair and should use it. The room was a stockroom with a window, which
is a luxury in the trade. Boxes with their flaps open, a kettle on a
box, a chair that had come from a kitchen. I read the first chapters of
what I had bought — a novel about a translator who begins to invent the
speeches she cannot find — and every few pages someone came through for
a title, and the owner would say, from the front, “It’s in the back,
don’t mind him.” I was him. I minded less than I expected. To be a piece
of furniture that does not have to move is, after a season of moving, a
kind of honour.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="40837" data-marxy-e="41695">The translator in the novel starts small: a softening, a courtesy, a
verb that is easier than the true one. Then she invents a whole replying
letter that the original speaker never wrote, because the silence on the
page feels, to her, like a failure of care. I closed the book at that
point, in the stockroom, and I thought of my cousin, and of the postcard
in the drawer, and of every dash this winter had offered me, and I
understood the temptation as if it were a weather I had stood in. To
finish other people’s sentences is a way of not being able to bear a
rag. The honest page leaves the short line. The honest letter leaves the
question about whether the winter was enough. I put the novel in my bag
and I went out through the shop, and the owner nodded without looking up
from a pile of returns, and the bell on the door did the small work
bells do.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="41697" data-marxy-e="41713">The last room</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="41715" data-marxy-e="42365">In the last week of February the thaw came, and with it a message from a
landlord who had found a set of keys and a date. I walked through the
empty flat on a morning when the pavements were shining in that flat way
that means the frost has given up. The rooms were nothing: white,
echoing, a window that looked onto a wall not unlike Mara’s, a kitchen
that had not yet learned anyone’s order of spices. I stood in the middle
of the floor and I clapped once, which is a thing I have seen people do
in empty rooms and have never understood until I did it. The sound went
nowhere interesting. It would, I thought, be a place I could fail to be
a guest.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="42367" data-marxy-e="43257">I did not move in that day. There was still a night at Mara’s, and a
return of a key to the Ellisons’ neighbour, and a bag to pack that had
acquired, over the winter, more books than I had arrived with, because a
town with a library and a bookshop will have its way with you. I sat at
Mara’s table for the last time with the chipped plate and the cheap wine
— a glass, not a ceremony — and I read the last chapter of the island
book, in which the walker admits that the two crossings cannot be
compared after all, because he was not the same walker, and the
admission is made without disappointment, which I liked. He sits on the
final ferry and watches the island become a shape, then a smudge, then a
line, and he does not take a photograph. I have tried to copy that
restraint here. I have no pictures of the rooms. I have the sentences,
which are a worse record and the one I trust.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="43259" data-marxy-e="43989">Mara, who had been kind enough not to ask what I was writing, asked that
night what I would do with a kitchen that was mine. I said I would put
the lamp from the spare room out of my head and buy a lamp that did not
make everything sepia. She said, “That’s a start.” Then she said,
“You’ll miss the drainpipe.” I told her I would not, and we both knew
this was a lie of the useful sort. I will miss the drainpipe. I will
miss the blue rectangle and the honest chapel light and the weak tea of
the carriage. I will miss being a temporary component in other people’s
systems. A person cannot live there forever. A person can know that the
forever rooms will have to work as hard as the borrowed ones did, or the
pages will go slack.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="43991" data-marxy-e="44861">On the morning I left I walked to the estuary with the last of the books
under my arm, the one I had reread in the clinic, and I stood where I
had stood with the cotton wool in my elbow, and the water was water
again, not metal. The gulls were fighting, which I took as a seasonal
correction. I did not open the book. I had no superstition left that
required a finished paragraph at a threshold of that size. I said, to no
one, that the winter had been enough, and then I took it back, because
enough is a word my cousin had used as a question, and I am not in a
position, even now, to make it a clean sentence. I walked to the new
door. I turned the new key. I set the bag of shirts on the floor and the
bag of books beside it, and I sat on the floor because there was no
chair yet, and I opened a page, and the page, which had been through
worse rooms than this, held.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="44863" data-marxy-e="45800">That is the end of the winter, and it is not the end of the habit. I
still read in kitchens after midnight, even when the kitchen is the one
with my own badly ordered spices. I still finish a paragraph on a
doorstep when the sentence is mid-stride. I still use letters as
bookmarks and promise to stop. I still notice the rag, the hole, the
word left on the platform. I still think of Helen-or-Mrs. Reed’s two
records, and of the lighthouse that does not care whether a ship is
there, and of the man on the sea wall who named the borrowed hour, and
of the woman in the clinic who knew what spaced-out sentences are for.
If I have a room of my own now, it is only so that I can remember the
others more clearly. The page was never the chapel. The page was the
thing I carried in, and carried out, and will carry again, the next time
a house is empty and a timer is ticking and a train is revising the
future in increments of four minutes.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="45802" data-marxy-e="45881">I have left the good plates where I found them. I have tried not to be a
guest.</p>
<h2 data-marxy-s="45883" data-marxy-e="45911">Asides the rooms required</h2>
<p data-marxy-s="45913" data-marxy-e="46154">What remains are the notes I would otherwise have put in the margin —
the asides that a borrowed room teaches you to keep quiet until the
owner is out. I have gathered them here so the winter can end without
pretending it had no underside.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="46156" data-marxy-e="46500">I have not named the novel I reread in the clinic because the hallway
chapter is the point, not the title, and because a title would send a
certain kind of reader out of this page and into a shop, which is a fine
thing to do with an afternoon and not the thing I am doing here. The
book exists. The hallway exists. The waiting room borrowed it.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="46502" data-marxy-e="46826">I have not described Mara’s father, or the north she went to, because
those rooms were not lent to me. A winter of borrowed rooms has an
ethics, even if it is only the ethics of not opening the third drawer
that sticks. She came back with less sleep and the same coat. That is as
much of her absence as this page is allowed.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="46828" data-marxy-e="47211">The Ellisons’ fish were still alive when I left, which is the only
success I am prepared to claim for the fortnight. Success is too large a
word. Continuity is better. A small system went on. The timer did not
need me, and I did not override it, and in that refusal I came as close
as I got all winter to being a good guest, which is to say a guest who
knows when to stop performing.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="47213" data-marxy-e="47902">If there is a method hidden in these rooms, it is only this: take the
page into the place that will not flatter it, and see whether the
sentences still stand. A clinic, a stalled train, a stockroom, a hostel
blanket, a kitchen that ticks. If they stand, they were built for more
than the furniture catalogue. If they fall over, you have learned
something cheaply that a quieter room would have charged you more for. I
have a preference, now, for pages that have already been through a
winter. I have a suspicion of the ones that look as if they have never
left a well-lit desk. This is unfair, and I stand by it, the way
Helen-or-Mrs. Reed stands by the little book as well as the machine.</p>
<p data-marxy-s="47904" data-marxy-e="48384">And if you, reading this in a room I cannot picture — a room with your
own lamp, or no lamp, or a child asking a question at the wrong time —
if you have stayed through the clinic and the drainpipe and the unsent
dash, then you have done the one thing a page can actually ask. You have
let a line turn when it had to. You have not required the winter to be a
clean sentence. That is enough to go on with, which is not the same as
enough, and is the only ending I can stand by.</p>
